Sodium-ion batteries are moving from validation to deployment, and durable full-cell operation is now limited less by bulk-material discovery than by the chemistry, structure, and mechanics of the nanoscale electrode–electrolyte interphases. Our new article in *Nanoscale Horizons* argues that the solid (SEI) and cathode (CEI) electrolyte interphases in Na-ion cells cannot be read as scaled lithium analogs — they are nanoscale, mosaic, dynamically reorganizing layers set by a Na⁺ solvation sheath and double-layer environment that differ markedly from Li⁺.

The article reframes Na-ion interphase design around three linked ideas:

- **Solvation-first:** the primary Na⁺ solvation sheath — not the bulk electrolyte-stability window — selects first-layer chemistry at the nanoscale, through adsorption, desolvation, and absorption at the electrode surface.
- **Cross-talk:** under lean electrolyte and practical negative-to-positive (N/P) ratios, the two interphases co-evolve through cross-talk rather than passivating independently.
- **Evidence tiers:** credible mechanistic claims require an explicit evidence-tier hierarchy and a minimum reporting set, so that comparisons across laboratories mean something.

The discussion is anchored in deployment-relevant markers — a representative hard-carbon full-cell initial coulombic efficiency near 91.4%, a reported optimal N/P of about 0.9 in a layered-oxide/hard-carbon pairing, and emerging 1.5 and 3.5 Ah cylindrical Na-ion formats — and closes with a co-design logic and benchmarking checklist for Na-ion full-cell interphase studies.
