Slide 6: Reaction extent keeps the atoms balanced

Recording 6:06 · Worksheet A1

Our reaction is A two gas in equilibrium with two A gas. We begin with one mole of A two and no A. No inert gas is present in the three required cases. A two contains two A atoms, so the reaction conserves atoms even though the number of gas molecules changes. Reaction extent tells us how far the reaction has proceeded on the written stoichiometric basis. Consuming one mole of A two produces two moles of A. The general expression is the initial amount plus the stoichiometric coefficient times the reaction extent. Reactant coefficients are negative and product coefficients are positive. For this particular feed, it is convenient to define f as the reaction extent divided by one mole. The fraction f is dimensionless. The amounts are one minus f moles of A two and two f moles of A. Nonnegative amounts require f between zero and one. These bounds come from the available material, before we use any equilibrium constant.

Slide 7: Activities make Q dimensionless

Recording 7:19 · Worksheet A2

To describe equilibrium, we need activities. For today's ideal gas model, the activity of a species is its mole fraction multiplied by total pressure, divided by the standard pressure. Both pressures must use the same units. Their ratio is dimensionless, and so is the activity. We use a standard pressure of one hundred kilopascals throughout this lesson. This is a reference convention. The actual total pressure is a separate input that we can change. For A two dissociating into two A, the quotient is the activity of A squared, divided by the activity of A two. The exponents come directly from the reaction coefficients. Write this activity expression before substituting mole fractions. That step makes it easier to see how pressure enters the calculation. Later we will compare one hundred and four hundred kilopascals at the same temperature. Our standard pressure stays at one hundred kilopascals in both cases.

Slide 10: Which way does the reaction proceed?

Recording 11:09 · Worksheet B1

How can the quotient tell us which direction is favored? At fixed temperature and pressure, the reaction Gibbs energy is the standard reaction Gibbs energy plus R T times the natural logarithm of Q. An equivalent form is R T times the natural logarithm of Q divided by K. A negative value favors forward reaction. A positive value favors reverse reaction. At an interior equilibrium, the reaction Gibbs energy is zero and Q equals K. These statements refer to the reaction as written. Reversing the reaction reverses the corresponding sign convention. For B one, use the supplied standard reaction Gibbs energy of minus four thousand joules per mole at three hundred and fifty kelvin. Test Q equal to one and Q equal to ten. Slide 25: Route 3: K at a new temperature

Recording 30:21 · Worksheet E1–E2

The integrated van't Hoff equation relates the natural logarithm of the ratio of the two equilibrium constants to the reaction enthalpy. With constant enthalpy, it is the enthalpy divided by R, multiplied by one over the reference temperature minus one over the new temperature.
