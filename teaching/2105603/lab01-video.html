Chapter 2: Set up the synthetic ideal mixture

Recording 1:13

Under Chemical system, choose Teaching binary, synthetic. Under Liquid model, keep Ideal solution. The extra symbols in this option belong to notation used later in the course. We only need the ideal assumption today. Enter three hundred fifty kelvin for Temperature, ninety-five kilopascals for Flash pressure, and zero point five for Feed fraction, z one. Then select Calculate equilibrium. The numerical inputs are preferable to the sliders when reproducing an exact case. At this temperature, the selected property record defines saturation pressures of one hundred fifty kilopascals for component A and sixty kilopascals for component B. These are synthetic teaching values, not measurements of a real chemical mixture. They come from the chosen system, so there are no separate saturation-pressure boxes to fill in for this case. Keep temperature and feed composition unchanged throughout the pressure comparison. On our one-mole feed basis, the feed contains half a mole of each component. Record the system, ideal assumption, temperature, pressure, and feed composition on guide page one.

Your action: Guide page 1. Teaching binary · synthetic; Ideal solution; T = 350 K; P = 95 kPa; z₁ = 0.5. Synthetic Psat₁ = 150 kPa and Psat₂ = 60 kPa at this T.

Chapter 4: Read the bubble and dew boundaries

Recording 3:49

Select P, x, y, fixed temperature in the Diagram menu. The horizontal axis is composition of component one. The vertical axis is pressure. The solid bubble curve uses liquid composition, x one. The dashed dew curve uses vapor composition, y one. The diamond marks the overall feed at the selected pressure. Look at the two boundary results below the diagram. Bubble pressure uses liquid composition equal to the feed composition, zero point five. Half of one hundred fifty plus half of sixty gives one hundred five kilopascals. This is where the first vapor appears when pressure is lowered from the liquid side. Dew pressure instead specifies vapor composition equal to zero point five. Its reciprocal is half divided by one hundred fifty, plus half divided by sixty. Taking the reciprocal gives approximately eighty-five point seven one four kilopascals. These boundary calculations answer different questions. Their specified compositions are not the two endpoint compositions of the current ninety-five-kilopascal tie line. Record both boundaries on guide page one and use them to classify our pressure cases.

Your action: Guide page 1. Bubble: x₁ = z₁ = 0.5, P = 105 kPa. Dew: y₁ = z₁ = 0.5, P = 85.7142857 kPa. P–x–y: solid bubble, dashed dew, diamond feed.

Chapter 5: Read the flash result at 95 kPa

Recording 5:17

Turn to guide page two. Our flash pressure is ninety-five kilopascals, between the dew and bubble pressures. The phase label therefore reads Liquid plus vapor. Read the three large results separately. Liquid x one is about zero point three eight eight nine. Vapor y one is about zero point six one four zero. Vapor fraction, beta, is about zero point four nine three five. The first two numbers describe composition within each phase. Beta describes how much of the entire feed becomes vapor. Beta is a molar amount fraction, not the vapor composition and not a volume fraction. On a one-mole feed basis, about zero point four nine three five moles are vapor, and zero point five zero six five moles are liquid. The horizontal tie line connects the liquid and vapor compositions at the same pressure. The feed composition lies between them. Component A is more concentrated in the vapor than in the liquid. Record the baseline in the guide, keeping enough digits for the balance check that follows.

Your action: Guide page 2. P = 95 kPa: liquid + vapor; x₁ = 0.388888889; y₁ = 0.614035088; β = 0.493506494. On F = 1 mol: L = 0.506493506 mol and V = 0.493506494 mol.

Chapter 6: Check both component balances independently

Recording 6:35

Now check the answer independently on guide page two. Start with one mole of feed, containing zero point five moles of A and zero point five moles of B. For component A, multiply the liquid amount by x one, then add the vapor amount multiplied by y one. The liquid contains about zero point one nine six nine seven moles of A. The vapor contains about zero point three zero three zero three moles. Their sum is zero point five moles. For component B, use one minus x one and one minus y one. The liquid contribution is about zero point three zero nine five two moles. The vapor contribution is about zero point one nine zero four eight moles. These also sum to zero point five moles. The two phase amounts must sum to one mole, and each phase composition must sum to one. Small differences from these totals can arise if you calculate with rounded screen values. Keep additional digits during the calculation. The lab's small residual checks its equations, while your hand calculation checks that you understand the quantities being balanced.

Your action: Guide page 2. A: 0.196969697 + 0.303030303 = 0.5 mol. B: 0.309523810 + 0.190476190 = 0.5 mol. L + V = 1 mol. Retain precision before rounding.

Chapter 7: Connect the Rachford–Rice root to the amount

Recording 7:57

For a closer look at the amount calculation, choose Rachford, Rice, vapor fraction in the Diagram menu. You may revisit this chapter later if you first want to complete the practical workflow. For this ideal calculation, each K value is its saturation pressure divided by the flash pressure. Using kilopascals for both numerator and denominator gives dimensionless values. K one is one hundred fifty divided by ninety-five, approximately one point five seven eight nine five. K two is sixty divided by ninety-five, approximately zero point six three one five eight. The plotted function combines both component balances. At beta equal to zero it is approximately positive zero point one zero five three. At beta equal to one it is approximately negative zero point one zero eight three. The crossing between them is the physical root. That root is beta approximately zero point four nine three five, the same vapor fraction we just checked on a one-mole feed basis. The horizontal axis here is vapor fraction, unlike the composition axis of the pressure diagram. Return the Diagram menu to P, x, y, fixed temperature before saving the baseline.

Your action: Guide page 2. K₁ = 150/95 = 1.578947368; K₂ = 60/95 = 0.631578947. F(0) = 0.105263158; F(1) = −0.108333333; F(β) ≈ 0 at β = 0.493506494. Return to P–x–y.

Chapter 14: Check the independent result

Recording 17:41

Now open guide page four and check your attempt. Ninety kilopascals lies between the dew and bubble pressures, so both liquid and vapor are present. From the ideal bubble relation, liquid x one equals ninety minus sixty, divided by one hundred fifty minus sixty. The result is one third. Vapor y one equals one hundred fifty times one third, divided by ninety, giving five ninths. The vapor fraction is the feed composition minus x one, divided by y one minus x one. This gives three quarters. On a one-mole feed basis, the liquid amount is one quarter of a mole and the vapor amount is three quarters. Component A contributes one twelfth of a mole in the liquid and five twelfths in the vapor, totaling half a mole. Component B contributes one sixth in the liquid and one third in the vapor, also totaling half a mole. If your answer differs, inspect the phase compositions and amount fraction separately. Retain your original attempt and correction. You have now completed the practical cycle: predict, calculate, check independently, save the evidence, and reopen it to reproduce the result.

Your action: Guide page 4, after attempting page 3. P = 90 kPa: x₁ = 1/3; y₁ = 5/9; β = 3/4; L = 1/4 mol; V = 3/4 mol. A balance: 1/12 + 5/12 = 1/2 mol. B balance: 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2 mol.
