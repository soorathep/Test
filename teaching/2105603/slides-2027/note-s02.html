Activity coefficients from measured VLE data

Pre-class note for Session 2 · about 20 minutes

Soorathep Kheawhom · 2105603 Advanced Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics

Draft for 2027. If you are taking the course this year, use the current course page.

Why read this. Session 2 takes a published data set for 2-propanol + water and asks how far a real liquid is from ideal, which model describes it, and how much the model can be trusted. This note sets up the vocabulary so the session can use its time on the data. Work the three questions at the end and bring one question of your own.

1. The working equation and what it assumes

Session 1 wrote equal fugacity in the γ–φ form. For component i in a liquid in equilibrium with its vapor,

y_i\,\Phi_i\,P = x_i\,\gamma_i\,P_i^{\text{sat}},\qquad \Phi_i=\frac{\hat\phi_i^{V}}{\phi_i^{\text{sat}}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{V_i^{L}(P-P_i^{\text{sat}})}{RT}\right].

Φi collects every vapor-phase and pressure correction. Solving for the activity coefficient gives the data-reduction equation

\gamma_i=\frac{y_i\,\Phi_i\,P}{x_i\,P_i^{\text{sat}}(T)}.

Three inputs carry the result, and each has its own error:

Input Comes from Typical problem
T, P, xi, yi the experiment scatter, and bias in the vapor sample
Pisat(T) a correlation (Antoine) wrong constants, units or range
Φi a vapor model (ideal gas or virial) neglected nonideality

When is Φi ≈ 1 acceptable? With the second virial coefficient and the Poynting term neglected, \ln\Phi_i\approx B_{ii}(P-P_i^{\text{sat}})/RT. For an illustrative B_{ii}=-1000\ \text{cm}^3\,\text{mol}^{-1} at 341 K and 60 kPa, the correction to ln γ is −0.011 for a component with Psat = 28.7 kPa and −0.0015 for one with Psat = 55.9 kPa. A 1 % shift is small next to the scatter of most data, but it is systematic: it has the same sign in every row, so it can bias a consistency test even when it is invisible in a plot.

2. Reducing one measured row

Row 7 of the 60 kPa data of Barbieri et al. (2024): T = 341.25 K, x₁ = 0.4943, y₁ = 0.569 (1 = 2-propanol, 2 = water). With the course Antoine constants (Section 6) and Φi = 1:

Psat / kPa γ ln γ
2-propanol 55.853 1.237 0.212
water 28.703 1.782 0.578

Two details matter. Psat is evaluated at the measured T of that row, because isobaric data change temperature from row to row. The pure-component rows (x₁ = 0 or 1) give 0/0 for the absent component and carry no γ for it. They are still useful: they test your Psat correlation against the authors’ own apparatus.

3. Why models are written for the excess Gibbs energy

The measured γ values are not independent. They are partial molar quantities of one function, the molar excess Gibbs energy:

g\equiv\frac{G^{E}}{RT}=\sum_i x_i\ln\gamma_i,\qquad \ln\gamma_i=\left(\frac{\partial\,(n\,g)}{\partial n_i}\right)_{T,P,n_{j\ne i}}.

For row 7, g = 0.4943(0.212) + 0.5057(0.578) = 0.397. Because γi derive from one function, they obey the Gibbs–Duhem equation at constant T and P:

x_1\,\mathrm{d}\ln\gamma_1+x_2\,\mathrm{d}\ln\gamma_2=0.

A model for g therefore gives both γ values consistently by construction. This is why fitting a GE model is better than fitting ln γ₁ and ln γ₂ with two unrelated curves. Common binary models:

Model Parameters Comment
Margules (one constant) A g = A x₁x₂; symmetric; a quick screen
Margules (two constant), van Laar 2 asymmetric; empirical
Wilson 2 (+ molar volumes) local composition; cannot predict liquid–liquid splitting
NRTL 2 + α local composition; can describe partial miscibility; used in Session 2

NRTL (Renon and Prausnitz, 1968) for a binary:

\ln\gamma_1=x_2^2\left[\tau_{21}\Big(\frac{G_{21}}{x_1+x_2G_{21}}\Big)^2+\frac{\tau_{12}G_{12}}{(x_2+x_1G_{12})^2}\right],\qquad G_{ij}=\exp(-\alpha\,\tau_{ij}).

In general τij depends on temperature, often as \tau_{ij}=a_{ij}+b_{ij}/T. Session 2 fits constant τ values. Keep this in mind when data at 60 and 80 kPa, which cover different temperature ranges, give different parameters.

4. What a fit is, and what it is not

A fit chooses parameters to minimize a sum of squared residuals. The choice of residual is part of the model statement:

  • ln γ residuals (used in Lab 02): simple and transparent, but they inherit the errors of y and Psat.
  • Pressure residuals, P only (Barker’s method, 1953): uses T, P, x and ignores y; useful when vapor sampling is the weakest measurement.
  • All variables with their uncertainties (maximum likelihood): the most defensible, and the most work.

Weights. A residual should count in proportion to how well the measured value is known. Propagating the uncertainties of x, y, P and T through the data-reduction equation gives, for component 1,

\sigma_{\ln\gamma_1}^2\approx\Big(\frac{u_x}{x_1}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{u_y}{y_1}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{u_P}{P}\Big)^2+\Big(\frac{\mathrm{d}\ln P_1^{\text{sat}}}{\mathrm{d}T}u_T\Big)^2 .

The first term grows without bound as x₁ → 0. In a data set with ux ≈ 0.01, ln γ₁ is known to a few hundredths at x₁ ≈ 0.5 but only to about ±1 at x₁ ≈ 0.01, yet an unweighted fit counts both residuals equally. Session 2 shows how much this choice moves the parameters.

Two points are easy to miss. First, NRTL parameters are strongly correlated: many (τ₁₂, τ₂₁) pairs give almost the same fitting error. Report the parameters with an uncertainty, or at least check how flat the minimum is. Second, a small fitting error shows that the model can follow these points. It does not show that the data are right, or that the model will work at other conditions. The honest test is a check on data the model has not seen, such as the other pressure in Session 2.

5. Consistency tests: what they can and cannot show

Measured T, P, x and y over-determine the problem, so Gibbs–Duhem can be used to test the data. The area test (Redlich and Kister, 1948; Herington, 1951) integrates it over the composition range. For isothermal data,

\int_0^1\ln\frac{\gamma_1}{\gamma_2}\,\mathrm{d}x_1=0.

For isobaric data the temperature changes along the integral, and the right-hand side becomes a heat-of-mixing term, -\int_0^1 \frac{H^{E}}{RT^2}\frac{\mathrm{d}T}{\mathrm{d}x_1}\,\mathrm{d}x_1, which is not zero. Applying the isothermal form to isobaric data can fail good data. Point tests compare the measured y with the y predicted from T, P, x through a GE model, row by row.

Passing a test does not certify the data. It shows that the data are consistent with Gibbs–Duhem within the tolerance chosen, under the assumed Φi and Psat. Failing a test can mean bad data, a wrong Psat correlation or a neglected vapor correction.

6. Vapor pressures: the input nobody measures for you

Every γ is divided by Psat, so a 3 % error in Psat becomes a 3 % error in γ (0.030 in ln γ), in every row and with the same sign. Before using any Antoine set, check four things: the log base, the pressure unit, the temperature unit, and the temperature range. Then compare it with the pure-component rows of the data.

7. Before Session 2

Write short answers. We use them in the first 30 minutes.

  1. Reduce a row. Repeat the row 7 calculation of Section 2 yourself, including g = GE/RT. Which component is further from ideal, and does that agree with which one is dilute?
  2. Propagate an error. Suppose the water Antoine set gave Psat 3 % too low. Which way does γ₂ move, by how much, and is the error random or systematic across the rows?
  3. Read a fit. An NRTL fit to the 60 kPa data has a small error. Give two separate reasons why the same parameters might still predict the 80 kPa data poorly.
