Gibbs energy of mixing and phase stability

Pre-class note for Session 3 · about 20 minutes

Soorathep Kheawhom · 2105603 Advanced Chemical Engineering Thermodynamics

Draft for 2027. If you are taking the course this year, use the current course page.

Why read this. Session 1 ended with a warning: when K depends on composition, a one-phase answer from a flash must be confirmed by a stability test. Session 2 ended with a fitted NRTL model for 2-propanol + water that matched the VLE data close to their stated uncertainty and still predicted a liquid split that does not exist. Session 3 asks when one liquid really splits into two, and how to test it. Work the three questions at the end and bring one question of your own.

1. The function that decides

At fixed T and P, a mixture takes the state of lowest total Gibbs energy. For a liquid, the molar Gibbs energy of mixing in units of RT is

\frac{\Delta g_{\text{mix}}}{RT}=\sum_i x_i\ln x_i+\frac{G^E}{RT}.

The ideal term is always negative and curved upward; it favors one mixed phase. A large positive GE can bend the curve the other way. If a straight line between two compositions lies below the curve, a feed between them lowers its Gibbs energy by splitting into those two liquids.

2. Local and global stability in a binary

Local test. One phase is stable against small composition changes when the curve is convex:

x_1x_2\,\frac{\partial^2(\Delta g_{\text{mix}}/RT)}{\partial x_1^2}=1+x_1x_2\,\frac{\partial^2 (G^E/RT)}{\partial x_1^2}>0.

This scaled curvature is the quantity reported in Labs 02 and 04. The spinodal is where it equals zero.

Global test. A positive curvature does not guarantee stability. The equilibrium compositions of the two liquids, the binodal, satisfy equal chemical potentials, which for a liquid–liquid split is

x_i^{\alpha}\gamma_i^{\alpha}=x_i^{\beta}\gamma_i^{\beta}\qquad(i=1,2).

Graphically this is the common tangent to Δgmix. Between the binodal and the spinodal the liquid is metastable: locally stable, but a split lowers G.

Worked example. For the one-constant Margules model, GE/RT = A x₁x₂, the curvature is 1 − 2A x₁x₂. It is smallest at x₁ = 0.5, so a split needs A > 2. By symmetry the binodal satisfies

\ln\frac{x_1}{1-x_1}=A\,(2x_1-1).

A Spinodal x₁ Binodal x₁
2.5 0.276 and 0.724 0.145 and 0.855
3.0 0.211 and 0.789 0.0707 and 0.929

At A = 3, a feed with z₁ = 0.12 has positive curvature but lies inside the binodal: it splits. This is the case you will meet first in Lab 04.

3. More than two components: the tangent-plane test

With C components the curvature becomes a matrix, and checking it is still only a local test. The practical global test is the tangent-plane distance (Michelsen, 1982). For a feed z and any trial composition w,

\frac{\text{TPD}(\mathbf w)}{RT}=\sum_i w_i\left[\ln w_i+\ln\gamma_i(\mathbf w)-\ln z_i-\ln\gamma_i(\mathbf z)\right].

If TPD ≥ 0 for every w, no second phase can lower the Gibbs energy and the feed is stable. If some w gives TPD < 0, the feed splits, and that w is a good initial guess for the new phase. In practice the minima of TPD are searched from several starts, because a search that finds no negative value has not proved that none exists. This is the test that the Session 1 flash needed before accepting a one-phase answer.

4. Which models can predict a split?

Not every GE model can. The Wilson equation keeps the curvature positive for any parameters, so it cannot describe liquid–liquid immiscibility however it is fitted. NRTL and UNIQUAC can. This flexibility has a cost: a model fitted only to VLE data can predict a split that does not occur.

Session 2 gave an example. Fitting NRTL (α = 0.47, constant τ) to the 2-propanol + water data at 60 kPa:

Fit τ₁₂ τ₂₁ Minimum scaled curvature
unweighted 0.362 1.752 0.238
weighted by measurement uncertainty 0.337 2.848 −0.030 near x₁ = 0.15

The weighted fit is the closer of the two to the stated measurement uncertainty (RMS of residual over σ 1.16, against 1.94), yet it predicts that a fully miscible liquid splits. VLE data near the dilute end constrain τ₂₁ weakly, and the phase behavior depends on it strongly. Mutual-solubility or LLE data, when they exist, are the evidence that rules such a model in or out.

5. From tie lines to parameters

When LLE is measured, each tie line gives two equations, x_i^{\alpha}\gamma_i^{\alpha}=x_i^{\beta}\gamma_i^{\beta}, so a single tie line at one temperature fixes two parameters. That is why a two-parameter fit can reproduce one tie line exactly, and why that is not evidence of a good model. Tie lines at several temperatures test whether constant τ is adequate; usually it is not, and τij(T) is needed. Lab 05 works through this.
