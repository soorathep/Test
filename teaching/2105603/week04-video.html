Slide 5: Process check

Recording 3:38 | Follow the study route and activity cues

Check your classifications. Dissolution of a crystal and sublimation transfer the same substance between bulk phases. Adsorption describes uptake associated with a surface. A chemical reaction would instead change species identities and require additional reaction constraints. The relevant energy criterion depends on what is fixed. At fixed temperature and pressure, a closed system minimizes Gibbs energy. At fixed temperature and volume, the corresponding potential is Helmholtz energy. In both cases, conserved inventories and the allowed states remain constraints on the minimization. For an ideal gas mixture, total pressure is the sum of partial pressures. An inert gas can therefore raise total pressure without changing the amount of the subliming species. Keep that distinction visible when we later interpret the Lab output.

Slide 6: Pure-solid standard state

Recording 4:41 | Follow the study route and activity cues

We begin with a solid and a liquid at equilibrium. The chemical potential of the component must be the same in the two present phases. On the liquid side, we write its chemical potential as the pure-liquid reference plus R T times the natural logarithm of x times gamma. The reference is the subcooled pure liquid at the same temperature and pressure. That reference may be a hypothetical continuation below the melting temperature, but it defines the activity convention consistently. The pure solid has unit activity on its own standard state. Unit activity does not mean that its chemical potential or amount is zero. The equality determines a liquid activity. Determining how many moles occupy each phase still requires a material balance.

Slide 7: Solubility equation

Recording 5:37 | Follow the study route and activity cues

The solubility equation links liquid activity to fusion properties. Read the right-hand side as negative fusion enthalpy divided by R, multiplied by the quantity one over T minus one over the melting temperature. That entire expression is dimensionless. Use temperature in kelvin and fusion enthalpy in joules per mole. With R in joules per mole per kelvin, the units cancel correctly. The expression assumes constant fusion enthalpy and neglects the heat-capacity correction between solid and liquid. It also assumes the specified pure solid, without solid solutions or chemical reactions. Below the melting temperature, the reciprocal-temperature difference is positive. The negative sign therefore gives a negative logarithm. For an ideal liquid, that predicts a saturated mole fraction below one.

Slide 10: Sublimation equilibrium

Recording 8:37 | Follow the study route and activity cues

Now consider a pure bulk solid in equilibrium with its vapor. Equality of chemical potential applies while both phases are present. With the ideal-gas and solid-pressure approximations stated here, this gives a vapor partial pressure equal to the sublimation pressure at that temperature. The phrase while solid remains matters. A small initial solid inventory may completely enter the gas phase before the vapor reaches that pressure. The final state can then have a lower partial pressure because there is no bulk solid left to enforce the two-phase equality. Also note which pressure appears in the condition. It is the partial pressure of the subliming species. An inert gas contributes to total pressure, but it is not part of that species' sublimation inventory.

Slide 11: Finite-vessel inventory

Recording 9:34 | Follow the study route and activity cues

Start the vessel calculation by finding the gas capacity at sublimation equilibrium. Multiply sublimation pressure by accessible gas volume, then divide by R T. Compare that capacity with the total amount of the subliming species. Gas moles equal the smaller of the available inventory and that capacity. Solid moles are the remaining inventory. Use gas moles in the ideal-gas equation to obtain the species partial pressure, and add the inert contribution only when calculating total pressure. With kilopascals and liters, R has the numerical value eight point three one four four six in compatible units. The gas chemical potential minus the pure-solid reference, divided by R T, is the natural logarithm of partial pressure divided by sublimation pressure. Zero applies with present solid. A negative value is allowed after solid exhaustion.

Slide 21: Adsorption and bulk phases

Recording 28:07 | Follow the study route and activity cues

Adsorption describes material associated with a surface. An adsorption isotherm gives uptake as a function of gas pressure at a specified temperature. In this lesson, q is absolute uptake measured in moles per kilogram of adsorbent. The pressure is the partial pressure of the adsorbate. To turn uptake into an amount in moles, multiply q by the adsorbent mass expressed in kilograms. For example, a value of one mole per kilogram on ten grams of adsorbent corresponds to zero point zero one mole adsorbed. That distinction will matter when we change adsorbent mass. A lower uptake per kilogram does not automatically mean fewer total adsorbed moles. The total amount depends on both uptake and the amount of adsorbent present.

Slide 22: Langmuir model

Recording 29:05 | Follow the study route and activity cues

The Langmuir equation writes uptake as the saturation uptake multiplied by b P, divided by one plus b P. Saturation uptake has units of moles per kilogram. The parameter b has reciprocal-pressure units, so the product b P is dimensionless. The ratio of uptake to saturation uptake is the fractional coverage in this model. It rises from zero toward one as pressure increases. At b P equal to one, the coverage is one half. The idealized interpretation involves a finite set of equivalent sites without lateral interactions. These assumptions help us understand the equation, but fitting the equation successfully does not establish a microscopic mechanism. Later, we will use it as a specified synthetic model and check its consequences through a vessel balance.

Slide 23: Henry limit

Recording 30:06 | Follow the study route and activity cues

At sufficiently low pressure, b P is much smaller than one. The denominator of the Langmuir equation is then approximately one, so uptake becomes saturation uptake times b times pressure. This gives the Henry adsorption coefficient as saturation uptake multiplied by b. With our chosen units, that coefficient is measured in moles per kilogram per kilopascal. A matched Henry line and Langmuir curve therefore share the same slope as pressure approaches zero. They do not necessarily agree at finite pressure. The word Henry also appears in gas-solubility equations, sometimes written as partial pressure equals a constant times liquid mole fraction. That convention defines a different constant. Always state the equation, phases and concentration basis rather than relying on the name alone.

Slide 27: Closed adsorption vessel

Recording 34:02 | Follow the study route and activity cues

For a closed adsorption vessel, total adsorbate inventory equals gas moles plus adsorbed moles. The gas term is pressure times accessible gas volume divided by R T. The adsorbed term is adsorbent mass multiplied by absolute uptake. Use the accessible gas volume in the ideal-gas term and convert adsorbent mass to kilograms before multiplying by uptake. The unknown pressure is the adsorbate partial pressure. At fixed temperature, volume, mass and inventory, the isotherm and this balance determine pressure together. For positive Langmuir parameters, both amount terms increase with nonnegative pressure, giving a single nonnegative solution. The balance uses absolute uptake. An excess-adsorption measurement needs a density and volume convention before it can replace that quantity.
