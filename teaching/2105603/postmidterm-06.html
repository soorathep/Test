Start with the chemical potential

Follow the same reaction throughout the derivation.

\mathrm{A_2(g)\rightleftharpoons 2A(g)}

The chemical potential of each species depends on its activity.

\mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln a_i

\mu_i^\circ refers to the chosen standard state. The activity a_i is dimensionless. For a gas, use a_i=f_i/p^\circ.

Combine the potentials along the reaction

At fixed temperature and pressure, a small forward reaction consumes one unit of A₂ and forms two units of A.

\mathrm dn_{\mathrm A}=2\,\mathrm d\xi,\qquad \mathrm dn_{\mathrm{A_2}}=-\mathrm d\xi

Substitute these amounts into \mathrm dG=\sum_i\mu_i\,\mathrm dn_i.

\mathrm dG=(2\mu_{\mathrm A}-\mu_{\mathrm{A_2}})\,\mathrm d\xi

The reaction Gibbs energy is the slope along the reaction extent \xi.

\Delta_rG=\left(\frac{\partial G}{\partial\xi}\right)_{T,P}=2\mu_{\mathrm A}-\mu_{\mathrm{A_2}}

The activity ratio is Q

Insert \mu_i=\mu_i^\circ+RT\ln a_i into the reaction Gibbs energy.

\Delta_rG=(2\mu_{\mathrm A}^\circ-\mu_{\mathrm{A_2}}^\circ)+RT(2\ln a_{\mathrm A}-\ln a_{\mathrm{A_2}})

Define \Delta_rG^\circ=2\mu_{\mathrm A}^\circ-\mu_{\mathrm{A_2}}^\circ and combine the logarithms.

\Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln\left(\frac{a_{\mathrm A}^2}{a_{\mathrm{A_2}}}\right)

The ratio calculated from the mixture’s current activities is the reaction quotient.

Q=\frac{a_{\mathrm A}^2}{a_{\mathrm{A_2}}},\qquad \Delta_rG=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln Q

At equilibrium, Q equals K

At reaction equilibrium, \Delta_rG=0. Call the equilibrium value of the activity ratio K.

0=\Delta_rG^\circ+RT\ln K,\qquad K=\exp\left(-\frac{\Delta_rG^\circ}{RT}\right)

For the written reaction and chosen standard states, the temperature determines K. Combine this relation with the expression for the current mixture.

\boxed{\Delta_rG=RT\ln\left(\frac{Q}{K}\right)}

Compare Q with K to predict the reaction direction. Which sign does \Delta_rG have when Q<K?

Q and K for the same reaction

For \mathrm{A_2(g)\rightleftharpoons 2A(g)}, use the dimensionless activity ratio a_{\mathrm A}^2/a_{\mathrm{A_2}}.

Reaction quotient, Q. Calculate the ratio using the activities in the mixture now.

Equilibrium constant, K. At equilibrium the ratio equals K. Its value is fixed at a given temperature for the written reaction and chosen standard states.

If Q<K If Q=K If Q>K
The forward reaction forms A. The reaction is at equilibrium. The reverse reaction forms A₂.

Compare the new quotient with K

At fixed mole fractions, the ideal-gas quotient for this reaction is proportional to pressure.

Q=\frac{y_{\mathrm A}^2}{y_{\mathrm{A_2}}}\frac{P}{p^\circ}

Before compression Immediately after Response at 350 K
Q=3.953 Q=7.906>K More A₂ forms.
Q=1.000 Q=2.000<K More A forms.

As the reaction proceeds, the mole fractions change. At the new equilibrium, Q=K=3.953 in both cases.

A real gas uses fugacity in each activity

At a specified T,P,\mathbf y, a mixture equation of state gives each component’s fugacity coefficient \phi_i. With an ideal-gas standard state,

a_i=\frac{f_i}{p^\circ}=\frac{\phi_i y_iP}{p^\circ},\qquad Q=\frac{a_{\mathrm A}^2}{a_{\mathrm{A_2}}}.

For this dissociation, the multiplier on the ideal-gas estimate is \phi_{\mathrm A}^2/\phi_{\mathrm{A_2}}. The coefficients can change during compression and as the reaction proceeds. The same T, written reaction and standard states give the same K.

Try it. At 350 K and 200 kPa, use y_{\mathrm A}=0.80, y_{\mathrm{A_2}}=0.20. For this illustrative state take \phi_{\mathrm A}=0.90 and \phi_{\mathrm{A_2}}=0.95. Calculate both activities and predict the direction.

Work the real-gas example on paper

The pressure ratio is 200/100=2. At the specified composition,

Quantity Calculation Value
a_{\mathrm A} 0.90(0.80)(2) 1.44
a_{\mathrm{A_2}} 0.95(0.20)(2) 0.38
Q 1.44^2/0.38 5.46

The ideal-gas estimate at this same state is 6.40. Both exceed K=3.953, so A₂ forms. To find the final extent for the real gas, update the composition and both fugacity coefficients until Q=K. Lab 12 solves the ideal-gas case.

The question for today

What determines the sign of a concentration-cell potential?

Week 5 gave us \Delta_rG=RT\ln(Q/K). A reversible cell turns that reaction driving force into a potential difference, E=-\Delta_rG/(nF).

Predict on paper. For the reaction as written, what sign should E have when Q<K? What voltage would you measure at complete cell equilibrium, Q=K?

Pre-class reading and preparation questions

Two reduction half-reactions

Enter both half-reactions as reductions. Reverse the left reaction and add the right after matching electron counts.

E_{cell}^\circ=E_{right}^\circ-E_{left}^\circ

Both potentials need the same reference electrode and temperature. Multiplying a half-reaction does not multiply its potential.

Nernst equation from reaction thermodynamics

Combine ΔrG=ΔrG°+RTlnQ with ΔrG=−nFE: E=E^\circ-\frac{RT}{nF}\ln Q

At 298.15 K, E=E^\circ-\frac{0.05915935\ \mathrm V}{n}\log_{10}Q

Changing T requires valid E° data at the new T.

A concentration-cell calculation

Use the same \mathrm{M^{2+}/M} couple on both sides at 298.15 K. For oxidation on the left and reduction on the right, Q=a_{\mathrm L}/a_{\mathrm R}. Take a_{\mathrm L}=0.01, a_{\mathrm R}=1, n=2, and neglect the junction potential. Q=0.01,\qquad E=+0.05915935\ \mathrm V

E°=0, K=1, but E is nonzero. Reversing the concentrations changes the sign of E. Equal activities give E=0.

ΔrG≈−11.416 kJ per mol of the written reaction.

An independent check

Use dimensionless activities. Concentration is an approximation with a stated standard concentration and activity coefficient.

Use Eright−Eleft and deltaG=−nFE with the balanced electron count. Equal activities must give zero potential for the same reduction couple.
