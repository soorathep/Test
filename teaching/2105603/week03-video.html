Slide 6: Gibbs–Duhem · 15–21 min

Recording 4:55 | Elapsed study time: 15-21 min

Now consider a binary liquid at fixed temperature and pressure. The Gibbs-Duhem equation connects changes in its two activity coefficients. If one coefficient changes with composition, the other cannot be chosen independently. Their mole-fraction-weighted logarithmic changes must sum to zero. This is a constraint on a collection of composition-dependent properties, rather than a test of one isolated gamma value. The second equation defines dimensionless excess Gibbs energy by weighting the two log activity coefficients. Under the same fixed conditions, the slope of that excess-energy curve equals the logarithm of gamma one divided by gamma two. That slope relationship will give us a local diagnostic. We can compare the slope inferred from the weighted properties with the log ratio at the same composition. Complete B one on page one. State the fixed conditions and explain the connection in one sentence.

Slide 7: The experimental path · 21–27 min

Recording 6:04 | Elapsed study time: 21-27 min

Experimental vapor-liquid data do not always follow a path with both temperature and pressure fixed. In an isothermal experiment, temperature stays constant while the equilibrium pressure generally changes with composition. The more general relationship therefore contains a pressure term involving molar excess volume. Our teaching calculation neglects that contribution. This approximation must accompany the interpretation. In an isobaric experiment, pressure is fixed but temperature changes. The local relationship then needs an excess-enthalpy correction. For real measurements, we need independent excess-enthalpy information before assigning that corrected local verdict. A missing correction is not evidence that the measurements failed. Lab Three also supplies synthetic excess enthalpy for its generated isobaric cases. That value comes from the generator; it is not an independent calorimetric measurement. Complete B two: identify the omitted pressure contribution and the property needed for the temperature correction.

Slide 8: Which method applies? · 27–35 min

Recording 7:23 | Elapsed study time: 27-35 min

The four approaches ask related but different questions. The isothermal area method integrates the log-activity ratio across the full composition range. Contributions of opposite sign can cancel. The local differential method compares a slope relationship at individual interior compositions, but numerical differentiation is sensitive to noise and spacing. Herington supplies an empirical allowance for an isobaric boiling-temperature range. That allowance does not replace measured excess enthalpy. The Van Ness-type approach asks whether a selected activity model can reproduce the observed pressure and vapor composition. Before interpreting any result, check whether the method applies to the data path and whether the required information is available. Not applicable, not assessed, and not run are not synonyms for failed. They are also not successful tests. Read the stated reason. Complete B three on page one, using guide page two if needed. Slide 19: Liquid stability · 100–105 min

Recording 19:27 | Elapsed study time: 100-105 min

At fixed temperature and pressure, stable equilibrium is the admissible state with the lowest total Gibbs energy, subject to the material balance. For a binary mixture, the mixing-energy curve contains the ideal mixing terms plus the excess contribution. This curve can become nonconvex even when the activity model satisfies the Gibbs-Duhem identity exactly. That is why consistency does not imply a stable homogeneous liquid. Equality of chemical potentials identifies candidate coexisting phases. We must still ask whether that candidate provides the lowest energy when all allowed compositions are considered. In a binary plot, a globally supporting common tangent supplies that comparison. The line represents the energy of a mixture of the two endpoint liquids. Complete F one on page three: explain what remains to be checked after a solver has found equal chemical potentials. Pause briefly before continuing to the geometric condition.

Slide 20: Common-tangent condition · 105–110 min

Recording 20:39 | Elapsed study time: 105-110 min

A common tangent touches the curve at two distinct compositions and has the same slope at both. Its slope must also equal the rise between the two energy values divided by the distance between the compositions. Those local equalities are necessary, but the word supporting adds a global requirement. Across the entire admitted composition range, the line must remain at or below the curve. If it crosses above the curve somewhere, another allowed state can lower the energy and the candidate is not globally stable. Similarly, a solution whose two endpoints coincide does not establish two distinct liquids. Positive curvature at one composition is only a local statement; it cannot by itself prove global stability. Keep this condition ready for F three. First, move to the next slide and attempt the lever-rule calculation in F two.
