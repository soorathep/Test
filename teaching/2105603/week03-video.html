Slide 6: Excess Gibbs energy · 15–40 min

Recording 4:55 | Elapsed study time: 15-40 min

To understand the constraint, we need one energy function. Excess Gibbs energy measures the difference between the actual mixture and an ideal mixture at the same temperature, pressure, and composition. It is not the entire Gibbs energy of mixing. The ideal contribution will also matter when we examine stability later. The partial molar excess Gibbs energy of a component is R T times the natural logarithm of its activity coefficient. The mixture's molar excess Gibbs energy is the composition-weighted sum of those partial molar quantities. Dividing by R T gives the dimensionless function shown here, which we will call g tilde. For a binary mixture, take x as the mole fraction of component one. Then g tilde equals x times log gamma one, plus one minus x times log gamma two. Every log here means the natural logarithm. The weights add to one. Both activity coefficients describe partial molar information from the same energy function. They therefore cannot be selected as two unrelated curves. Use this definition to begin B one.

Slide 7: Gibbs–Duhem · 15–40 min

Recording 6:17 | Elapsed study time: 15-40 min

At fixed temperature and pressure, the Gibbs-Duhem equation tells us how the two activity coefficients must change together. Multiply the change in log gamma one by x. Multiply the change in log gamma two by one minus x. The sum must be zero. These are composition-weighted changes, not an instruction that the activity coefficients themselves must add to one. At an interior composition, if one log activity coefficient increases as composition changes, the other must change in a compatible way. The weights determine the relationship between those changes. An ideal mixture is a simple special case. Both gamma values equal one, both logarithms are zero, and the condition is satisfied. But a nonideal mixture can also satisfy Gibbs-Duhem. Large gamma values do not, by themselves, indicate inconsistent data. What matters is the relationship between their composition dependences.

Slide 8: From excess energy to a slope · 15–40 min

Recording 7:26 | Elapsed study time: 15-40 min

Now differentiate g tilde with respect to composition. Use the product rule: both the weights and the logarithms depend on x. Differentiating x times log gamma one produces log gamma one, plus x times the derivative of log gamma one. Differentiating one minus x times log gamma two produces minus log gamma two, plus one minus x times the derivative of log gamma two. Collect the two plain logarithms first. Their difference is the natural logarithm of gamma one divided by gamma two. We call that quantity L. The remaining terms are the composition-weighted derivatives. At fixed temperature and pressure, Gibbs-Duhem makes their sum zero. The result is compact: the slope of g tilde equals L at the same composition. This gives us two ways to describe the same slope. We can estimate it from neighboring excess-energy values, or calculate L from the activity coefficients at the center. Their difference becomes our local residual. Write the product-rule steps in B one, rather than jumping straight to the final equation.

Slide 9: Follow the experimental path · 15–40 min

Recording 8:46 | Elapsed study time: 15-40 min

Before applying that result to measurements, ask how the measurements were collected. Temperature, pressure, or both may change. Along a general path, the weighted changes in log gamma include two additional terms. The temperature term is minus the molar excess enthalpy divided by R times temperature squared, multiplied by the temperature change. The pressure term is molar excess volume divided by R T, multiplied by the pressure change. When both temperature and pressure are fixed, both terms vanish. For isothermal vapor-liquid equilibrium, pressure usually varies across the composition range. Lab zero three neglects the excess-volume pressure contribution. That is an assumption to state, not an exact consequence of holding temperature fixed. For isobaric data, temperature varies. The excess-enthalpy correction is then needed for the local test. Real data without independent excess-enthalpy information cannot receive this lab's local consistency verdict. A synthetic generator can supply its own correction, but that is not independent calorimetry.

Slide 10: Match the method to the claim · 15–40 min

Recording 10:07 | Elapsed study time: 15-40 min

The methods in this table examine different evidence. The isothermal area method tests an integral over the full composition range. It needs suitable endpoint information and the stated pressure approximation. Positive and negative departures can cancel in that integral. The local differential method examines the slope relationship across composition. It can reveal a localized departure, but differentiation also makes it sensitive to point spacing, rounding, and noise. Its path corrections must be appropriate. Herington uses an empirical area allowance for an isobaric boiling-temperature range. It does not provide measured excess enthalpy. A Van Ness-type test instead examines how well a selected model reproduces pressure and vapor composition. That result depends on the chosen model and the property assumptions. Use precise status words. Not applicable means the method does not match the situation. Not assessed can mean required information is missing. Not run means the calculation has not been performed. Outside this criterion means a calculated diagnostic exceeds its stated limit. In B three, identify missing information before interpreting a number.

Slide 11: What a local residual measures · 15–40 min

Recording 11:37 | Elapsed study time: 15-40 min

Here is the local calculation we will actually use. First, calculate g tilde at every composition using the weighted log activity coefficients. Second, estimate its slope from neighboring points. Third, subtract L, the center-point log gamma one minus log gamma two. That difference is the residual r, under fixed conditions or the stated isothermal approximation. The residual is dimensionless. A positive value means the estimated slope exceeds the center log ratio; a negative value means it is smaller. Its sign alone does not identify a physical cause. Lab zero three summarizes the absolute residuals over interior points by taking their mean and multiplying by one hundred. The teaching threshold for that scaled score is five. It is not a probability or a percentage of incorrect measurements. One residual is also not the full-data average. Our lab uses three-point finite differences, rather than the NIST or Kojima point test based on a Padé representation. Pause now. Slide 29: Consistency does not imply one stable liquid · 105–155 min

Recording 36:30 | Elapsed study time: 105-155 min

We now change the question. Consistency asks whether the composition dependence of the properties obeys a thermodynamic relationship. Stability asks which admissible arrangement of phases has the lowest total Gibbs energy. At fixed temperature and pressure, compare one homogeneous liquid with possible splits that preserve the total amount of each component. The mixing Gibbs energy contains the ideal mixing terms, x log x and one minus x times log one minus x, plus the excess Gibbs energy. A sufficiently unfavorable excess contribution can make the total curve nonconvex. The homogeneous liquid can then sit above a lower-energy two-liquid mixture. Nothing in that statement requires the activity model to violate Gibbs-Duhem. Indeed, a model derived consistently from one excess Gibbs-energy function can predict liquid-liquid separation. Slide 30: What local curvature tells us · 105–155 min

Recording 37:46 | Elapsed study time: 105-155 min

Local curvature describes how the homogeneous liquid responds to a small composition fluctuation. Positive curvature means that sufficiently small fluctuations increase its bulk Gibbs energy. Negative curvature means that small fluctuations can lower the energy. However, a small-fluctuation test does not compare every possible finite separation. That distinction separates stable and metastable homogeneous liquids. A stable liquid lies on the lower convex envelope. A metastable liquid has positive curvature but lies above a lower-energy combination of two liquids. An unstable liquid has negative curvature and also fails the global minimum requirement. For the one-parameter Margules model, the dimensionless excess Gibbs energy is A times x times one minus x. The curvature of the total mixing function is one divided by the product of x and one minus x, then subtract two times A. We will use A equal to three. This bulk equilibrium model does not calculate nucleation barriers, interfaces, or how long a metastable liquid will persist.

Slide 31: A common tangent must support the curve · 105–155 min

Recording 39:10 | Elapsed study time: 105-155 min

Imagine joining two points on the Gibbs-energy curve with a straight line. The energy of a mixture of those two liquid compositions lies on that line, because the total molar energy is the phase-fraction-weighted average. At a candidate equilibrium pair, the line has the same slope as the curve at both ends. That common-tangent condition corresponds to equality of the component chemical potentials. But finding a stationary pair is only part of the calculation. The tangent must support the whole admitted curve: nowhere may another composition lie below it. If the candidate line crosses above the curve, another admissible state can lower the energy and the candidate is not globally stable. Also check that the two compositions are distinct. A solver returning the same composition twice has not established two liquids.
