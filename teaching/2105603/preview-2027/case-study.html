2. Calculate activity coefficients from that measurement

Ideal vapor · negligible pressure correction

Find each pure-component saturation pressure at the measured temperature. Then use the measured x1, y1 and P to infer γ1 and γ2. An activity coefficient describes departure from ideal-liquid behavior; γ = 1 is the ideal-liquid limit.

γ1=y1Px1P1sat
γ2=(1−y1)P(1−x1)P2sat
Use the same pressure unit for P and both Psat values. Activity coefficients γ are dimensionless.
Get Psat from the Antoine equation
log10(Psatbar)=A−BT+C

Use T in K. The equation gives Psat in bar. Multiply by 100 to convert bar to kPa. The pressure is the equilibrium pressure of each pure liquid with its own saturated vapor.

    How is the error calculated?

    RMSE=∑j=1N(r1,j2+r2,j2)2N

    Each residual is the model value of ln γ minus the value inferred from the same measurement. The index j identifies the observation, and N is the number of usable measurement pairs. RMSE and ln γ are dimensionless.

      A small fitting error does not establish experimental thermodynamic consistency. The measurements follow a changing-temperature, constant-pressure path. Continue to Lab 03 to study consistency-test assumptions.

      A. What does γ > 1 tell you about this mixture?

      Use the starting measurement (x₁ = 0.4943 at 341.25 K) and calculate γ₁ and γ₂ in Step 2.

      Compare with the explanation

      With P₁sat = 55.85 kPa and P₂sat = 28.70 kPa at 341.25 K, γ₁ = 1.24 and γ₂ = 1.78.

      Both exceed 1: a positive deviation from Raoult's law. Each component escapes to the vapor more readily than in an ideal solution because unlike pairs (2-propanol with water) interact less favorably than like pairs. The mixture boils at a higher pressure, or a lower temperature, than Raoult's law predicts.

      B. Where is the azeotrope, and why does it occur?

      In Step 1, find the 60 kPa row where y₁ is closest to x₁. Compare its temperature with the other rows.

      Compare with the explanation

      At x₁ = 0.6461, y₁ = 0.6492, and T = 340.92 K is the lowest temperature in the table: a minimum-boiling azeotrope.

      At an azeotrope yi = xi, so γ₁P₁sat = γ₂P₂sat = P and γ₁/γ₂ = P₂sat/P₁sat = 28.29/55.04 = 0.514. The data give γ₁/γ₂ = 0.521. Simple distillation cannot cross this composition.

      C. Why are the pure-component rows excluded from activity inference?

      Look at the rows with x₁ = 1 and x₁ = 0 in Step 1.

      Compare with the explanation

      At x₁ = 1, the absent component has x₂ = y₂ = 0, so γ₂ = (1 − y₁)P/[(1 − x₁)P₂sat] = 0/0 is undefined. The present component has γ₁ = 1 by the pure-liquid standard state, which carries no information about the interaction.

      The dilute limit γ∞ has to be approached from the nearby interior rows, not read from the endpoint.

      What these results can and cannot show

      • The ± bounds you enter are illustrative perturbations, not the article's pointwise standard uncertainties. No distribution, covariance or probability is assigned.
      • A sampled range covers only the accepted scenarios. It is not a confidence interval or a proven bound on every state inside the box.
      • The fixed-α fits are conditional two-parameter fits. They do not jointly estimate α, give parameter confidence intervals, bootstrap measurement uncertainty or prove non-identifiability.
      • Your retained model and the source measurements are never replaced by these investigations.

      Methods and limits of this case study

      Activity inference assumes ideal vapor, negligible pressure corrections and pure-liquid standard states. The teaching fit holds τ12 and τ21 constant across the selected temperature range. It minimizes an unweighted mean squared ln γ residual and does not estimate parameter confidence intervals.

      The article reference uses τ = a + b/T. This equation requires b in K, although Table 7 prints 1/K. The numerical values are retained with the article's equation. This exercise uses different property assumptions and only the 40 newly reported observations, so it does not reproduce the article's wider Aspen regression.

      The experimental path is isobaric and temperature varies with composition. An independent consistency verdict would need appropriate endpoint treatment and the temperature correction involving excess enthalpy. Fitting errors and a numerical Gibbs–Duhem check of NRTL do not supply that verdict.
