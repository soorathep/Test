Lab 13 · Electrochemical equilibrium

Why does a cell with identical electrodes have a voltage?

Two compartments each hold a synthetic metal electrode M(s) in a solution of M²⁺, joined by a salt bridge. The left solution is 0.01 mol L⁻¹ and the right 1.0 mol L⁻¹, at 298.15 K. Both electrodes have the same standard potential, so E° = 0.

Calculate the cell voltage and identify which electrode is the cathode. Then explain where the electrical work comes from.

Model and validity

Both electrodes are the same pure metal, so E° = 0 at every temperature. The liquid-junction potential across the salt bridge is neglected. Single-ion activity coefficients cannot be measured on their own; the supplied values are modelling assumptions.

    A. Calculate E on paper

    Write the cell reaction for left oxidation and right reduction, then apply the Nernst equation with E° = 0.

    Compare with the explanation

    Overall: M²⁺(R) → M²⁺(L), with n = 2. Q = aL/aR = 0.01, so E = E° − (RT/2F) ln Q = −(0.01285 V)(−4.605) = 0.0592 V.

    Equivalently, 29.6 mV for each factor of ten in the activity ratio, and the ratio here is two decades. E is positive, so the reaction runs as written: the right electrode is the cathode.

    B. What sets the sign and the size of E?

    Compare “Concentrations exchanged”, “Equal concentrations” and “Same ratio · 0.001 and 0.1”.

    Compare with the explanation

    Exchanging the solutions gives −59.2 mV: the same reaction now runs in reverse, and the left electrode becomes the cathode. Equal concentrations give 0, because Q = K = 1. The 0.001 and 0.1 pair gives 59.2 mV again.

    E depends only on the activity ratio, not on the absolute concentrations. The more concentrated side is always the cathode, because deposition there lowers its activity.

    C. Where does the electrical work come from?

    Read ΔG for the starting case. ΔG° = 0 here, so no chemical change occurs overall.

    Compare with the explanation

    ΔG = −2 × 96 485 × 0.0592 = −11.4 kJ per mol of M²⁺ transferred, equal to RT ln(aL/aR) for moving one mole of ions from the 1.0 to the 0.01 mol L⁻¹ solution.

    The work comes from the free energy of dilution, which is entropic for ideal solutions. Mixing the two solutions directly would waste it; the cell converts it into electrical work.

    What these results can and cannot show

    • Single-ion activity coefficients are not measurable. Debye–Hückel estimates are reliable only at low ionic strength.
    • A real salt bridge adds a liquid-junction potential, often a few millivolts, which is comparable to the corrections here.
    • The discharge study assumes equal volumes, constant γ and reversible operation. A real cell loses part of the work to resistance and overpotential.
