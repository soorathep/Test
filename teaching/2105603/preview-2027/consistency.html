Work through the equations before making a claim

Area: an integrated requirement

I=∫01ln(γ1γ2)dx1D=100|A+−A−|A++A−
  1. Integrate the log ratio over the full composition range.
  2. Separate positive area A+ and the magnitude of negative area A−. Segments are split at zero crossings.
  3. Compute D. If A+ = 0.40 and A− = 0.39, then I = 0.01 and D = 100(0.01)/0.79 = 1.27%.
  4. D < 5% meets this empirical isothermal criterion. It cannot rule out canceling local errors. If both areas vanish, D is undefined.

Differential: a local requirement

g=gERT=x1lnγ1+(1−x1)lnγ2
r=dgdx1−ln(γ1γ2)+HERT2dTdx1

At fixed T, the heat term is zero; the excess-volume pressure term is neglected. At fixed P, the displayed heat term is needed. Three-point derivatives use actual composition spacing. The teaching score is 100 mean |r|, with a threshold of 5. It is not a percentage uncertainty or the NIST/Kojima Padé point test.

Herington: an empirical allowance

For an isobaric path, J = 150(Tmax − Tmin)/Tmin, with temperatures in K. This lab evaluates D − J < 10 only when a full-range area is available. J is not a measured excess-enthalpy correction. This is one published criterion variant, not the complete NIST TDE decision tree.

Modeling: reproduction by a chosen model

The NRTL teaching fit holds α = 0.47. It uses two constant parameters on an isothermal path, or four temperature-dependent parameters on an isobaric path. Thresholds are mean absolute relative pressure error < 1% and mean absolute vapor-composition error < 0.01 mol/mol. Different model flexibility, property errors or numerical convergence can change the result. This is not the full NIST five-parameter Van Ness implementation.

Write first, then compare

Use the calculations above to answer each question in your own words. Then open the explanation and compare. Your answers are saved with your study.

A. What does the Gibbs–Duhem equation require of ln γ₁ and ln γ₂?

Calculate case A and look at the area and local plots in Step 2.

Compare with the explanation

At fixed T and P, x₁ d ln γ₁ + x₂ d ln γ₂ = 0. The two activity coefficients cannot vary independently: where ln γ₁ rises with x₁, ln γ₂ must fall, in the ratio −x₁/x₂.

Measured γ₁ and γ₂ come from separate measurements, so a consistency test asks whether they obey this constraint. Case A was generated from one Gibbs-energy model, so it satisfies it.

B. Why does case B pass the area test but fail the local test?

Load B · Canceling error in Step 1, calculate, and compare the two criteria with case A.

Compare with the explanation

Case B passes the area criterion (D ≈ 0.02%, below 5%) but fails the local criterion (100·mean|r| ≈ 15.8, above 5).

The imposed error f = a cos(2πx₁) integrates to zero over the full composition range, so the area I is unchanged. Locally, the residual changes by −f. An integral test is necessary for consistency, but it cannot detect errors that cancel.

C. Why does the isothermal area test not apply to isobaric data?

Select the measured 60 kPa case and read the assumptions listed under Step 1.

Compare with the explanation

At fixed pressure, the boiling temperature changes with composition. The area integral then equals a term containing the excess enthalpy, ∫(HE/RT²)(dT/dx₁)dx₁, which is not zero in general.

Without measured HE, an exact verdict is not possible. The Herington allowance J is an empirical substitute for that missing term.

Why the canceling case is a useful trap

Let f(x1) = a cos(2πx1). Add (1 − x1)f to ln γ1 and subtract x1f from ln γ2. The weighted sum g stays unchanged, while ln(γ1/γ2) increases by f.

  1. The full-range integral of cos(2πx1) is zero, so the imposed error cancels in I.
  2. The local residual changes by −f, up to differentiation error. At x1 = 0.5 and a = 0.25, f = −0.25, so the added local residual is +0.25.
  3. Therefore a small area does not establish the local partial-molar relationship. Use the calculated numbers to test this reasoning.

Why can a pressure error be invisible to the area?
ln(γ1γ2)=ln(y11−y1)−ln(x11−x1)−ln(P1satP2sat)

The same measured P multiplies both inferred activity coefficients, so it cancels from their ratio under the adopted ideal-vapor and pressure-correction approximations. A common pressure calibration shift changes each γ, while leaving this log ratio unchanged. Compare the P-shift rows. An insensitive area does not establish an accurate pressure measurement. Vapor fugacity or liquid pressure corrections can break this simple invariance.
