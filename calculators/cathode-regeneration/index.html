Compare immediate regeneration with continued use

Compare the undiscounted two-life cost of immediate regeneration (B) with continued use followed by virgin replacement (D). Enter regeneration cost and a matched regenerated-to-pristine cycle-life factor. The reference model converts cycle life to energy under explicit ageing and service-life assumptions.

Regenerate at retirement (B) beats continued use, then virgin replacement (D), when  κ < λE + p(1 + λE)/2 − (1 + κ)vρE/2

How to use

  1. Choose the case. Pick the cathode route, the state of health at which the cell is retired, and the boundary: CAM counts the cathode material only; Cell adds the non-CAM cell cost and the cost of repurposing the old cell. The other fields then load the reference values of the model.
  2. Enter your process. Regeneration cost in USD per kg CAM (processing, reagents and energy), and the cycle-life factor f: equivalent full cycles to 80% capacity of the regenerated material divided by that of pristine material, measured in full cells under the same protocol. Example: pristine 1,000 EFC, regenerated 900 EFC, enter 0.90. The model converts f to the delivered-energy ratio λE; these are distinct quantities.
  3. Read the verdict. Read the named comparator and the decision margin. A positive margin favors regeneration (B) in this undiscounted two-life comparison per unit of service-weighted energy. If continued use is precluded by the service-life cap, the comparator is virgin replacement (A). The tiles show the cycle-life and regeneration-cost thresholds; a service-cap limit can make a strict B advantage unattainable.
  4. Change assumptions only if you have better data. Open Market and use assumptions to change the value of second-use energy, the repurposing cost, the virgin CAM cost, the non-CAM cell cost or the continuation energy. Reset restores the reference case.

Scope. This compares two-life manufacturing and end-of-life costs per service-weighted energy. Charging electricity and operating costs are excluded. First-life costs and energy remain in the ratio; v weights continued-use energy. The result does not identify the best of all four strategies or a fleet operator's incremental decision at retirement.
Reference. Revised reference data are pending.
Discounting. The displayed rule is undiscounted; no accuracy percentage or universal decision-margin bound is assumed.
Numerical precision. Reference cost inputs are displayed to two decimal places and ρE to four. The energy lookup uses piecewise linear interpolation, without extrapolation. Rounding and interpolation can affect comparisons close to zero; use unrounded inputs and the full timeline when the difference matters.
Cycle life and energy. The entered f is converted to λE = Ereg/E1 using the reference fade law, calendar ageing and service-life cap. The editable ρE = Econt/E1 is distinct from the empirical throughput input ρEFC. No extra repair penalty is added to f. Targets beyond the lookup grid are not extrapolated. A target above the service-cap energy bound is unattainable; a target at that bound permits at most equality, not a strict B advantage.
Conversion assumption. Nominal CAM specific energy and voltage are held equal for pristine and regenerated lives. Initial capacity recovery and round-trip efficiency are not varied independently. Use a matched nominal-energy basis for the measured life factor f.
Published ratios. Shading shows illustrative reported ranges (0.16–0.61 and 0.90–1.03) from studies with different chemistries, cell formats and protocols (ESI Table S4). These ranges are not a probability distribution or a guarantee of structural repair.
Boundary. One regeneration loop and a 60% SOH continuation floor are represented. If the service-life cap precludes continuation, the comparator becomes virgin replacement (A). Pack integration, application-specific revenue, failure risk, residual value and a full environmental inventory are outside this calculator.
