How far does a gas reaction go?

A synthetic dimer dissociates in the gas phase, A₂ ⇌ 2A. At 350 K, ΔG° = −4.0 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔH° = +50 kJ mol⁻¹ with a standard pressure of 1 bar. One mole of A₂ is fed at 350 K and 100 kPa, and the gases are ideal.

Find the extent of reaction and the equilibrium composition. Then predict how pressure, an inert gas and temperature move it.

Model and validity

One reaction among ideal gases, with activities aᵢ = yᵢP/P° and P° = 100 kPa. The inert gas is added at constant total pressure. The extent is found from ln Q(ξ) = ln K, which has a single root because G is convex along the reaction. Temperatures are limited to 250–650 K and pressures to 5000 kPa.

    A. Solve the starting case on paper

    Calculate K from ΔG°, write the mole fractions in terms of ξ, and solve for ξ.

    Compare with the explanation

    K = exp(4000/(8.314 × 350)) = 3.95. With nA₂ = 1 − ξ, nA = 2ξ and ntotal = 1 + ξ, the condition is K = 4ξ²/(1 − ξ²) × P/P°. At P = P°, ξ² = 3.95/7.95, so ξ = 0.705 and yA = 1.41/1.705 = 0.827.

    A negative ΔG° means K > 1, not complete reaction. The equilibrium mixture still contains 0.295 mol of A₂.

    B. Why do pressure and an inert gas change the result?

    Compare the starting case with “Ten times the pressure” and “Add 4 mol inert gas”. Read K in each case.

    Compare with the explanation

    K stays 3.95 in all three cases. At 1000 kPa, ξ falls to 0.300; with 4 mol inert at 100 kPa, ξ rises to 0.870.

    The reaction makes one extra mole of gas (Δν = +1), so Q contains the factor P/P° × 1/ntotal. Higher pressure raises Q, so the reaction shifts back. An inert gas at constant total pressure lowers the partial pressures, so the reaction shifts forward. In a rigid vessel, adding an inert gas would leave the partial pressures and ξ unchanged.

    C. What does temperature do to K?

    Load “Heated to 400 K” and compare K and ξ. Then calculate 300 K.

    Compare with the explanation

    ln K(400) = ln K(350) + (ΔH°/R)(1/350 − 1/400) = 1.375 + 2.148 = 3.522, so K = 33.9 and ξ = 0.946. At 300 K, K = 0.226 and ξ = 0.231.

    The dissociation is endothermic, so K rises with temperature. Unlike pressure, temperature changes K itself, because ΔG° depends on T.

    What these results can and cannot show

    • The reaction data are synthetic. Real K values come from calorimetry, formation data or measured compositions, each with its own uncertainty.
    • Equilibrium says how far a reaction can go, not how fast. A slow reaction may never reach this state.
    • At high pressure, fugacity coefficients replace yᵢP. The ideal-gas result is then a first estimate only.
