Model assumptions

The liquid Gibbs energy uses the selected activity model; the vapor is ideal. Both are placed on one Gibbs-energy reference using the pure-component saturation pressures, so the stable phases are those on the lowest common tangent of the liquid and vapor curves. Poynting corrections and temperature dependence are neglected. The system is hypothetical and does not represent a named chemical pair.

    4. Check your understanding

    Write first, then compare

    Use Steps 1–3 to answer each question in your own words. Then open the explanation and compare. Your answers are saved with your study.

    A. Why are the three compositions the same for every feed at P₃?

    Calculate the starting case (z₁ = 0.50) and “Same pressure · z₁ = 0.20”. Compare x₁ᵅ, x₁ᵝ and y₁.

    Compare with the explanation

    x₁ᵅ = 0.0707, x₁ᵝ = 0.9293 and y₁ = 0.6667 are identical for both feeds. Only the amounts change.

    For two components and three phases, F = 2 − 3 + 2 = 1. Fixing T uses that one degree of freedom, so P₃ and all three compositions are fixed. This extends Labs 01 and 04: compositions come from the equilibrium conditions, amounts come from the material balance.

    B. Why are the three amounts not unique?

    At the starting case, move the slider under the phase table and watch the three amounts.

    Compare with the explanation

    There are three unknown amounts but only two balances: total moles, fα + fβ + fV = 1, and component 1, fαx₁ᵅ + fβx₁ᵝ + fVy₁ = z₁. For z₁ = 0.50 any vapor amount from 0 to 0.72 mol satisfies both.

    The phase rule fixes intensive variables only. One more extensive specification, such as the total enthalpy or volume, is needed to choose the amounts. Lab 07 supplies it with an energy balance.

    C. Why do only two phases remain above or below P₃?

    Calculate “Higher pressure · 140 kPa” and “Lower pressure · 100 kPa”, and compare the Gibbs curves in Step 3 with the starting case.

    Compare with the explanation

    The ideal-vapor Gibbs energy contains ln(P/Pisat), so raising P lifts the vapor curve while the liquid curve hardly changes. At 140 kPa the vapor curve lies above the liquid common tangent: two liquids, no vapor. At 100 kPa it drops below that tangent, and a new tangent between one liquid and the vapor gives liquid + vapor (V ≈ 0.79 mol).

    Only at P₃ does one straight line touch both liquid minima and the vapor curve at once.

    What these results can and cannot show

    • The system is hypothetical: one fixed temperature, constant liquid parameters, an ideal vapor and assumed saturation pressures.
    • Equilibrium decides which phases are present and their compositions. It does not decide the amounts at P₃, and it does not predict how fast phases separate.
    • A vapor-fraction specification here stands in for an energy or volume balance; it is not calculated from heat input.
